🔄 Reverse Charades

The whole team acts out a scene together while one person guesses. Great for icebreakers, classrooms and large groups.

In classic charades, one performer carries the whole turn while everyone else watches. Reverse Charades flips that around: one player sits in the hot seat as the guesser, and the rest of the team (three to seven people works best) acts out a scene together in silence.

The change makes the game friendlier. Shy players and younger children who dislike being on stage alone can blend into the group, and the guesser gets a whole scene of clues instead of one person's gestures.

Classic vs. Reverse Charades

MechanicStandard CharadesReverse Charades
ActingOne performer acts alone.The whole team acts together.
GuessingThe audience calls out guesses.One player guesses.
Best promptsWords, titles and idioms.Scenes, places and situations.
PressureAll on one person.Shared across the team.
Typical turn30 to 60 seconds per card.60 seconds per scene, or per sprint of several scenes.

How to Play

  1. Pick the guesser. One person sits facing the team. Everyone else is an actor. Aim for four to seven actors; split bigger groups into two teams.
  2. Draw a scene. Use the picker below, or point to a line on a printed sheet. The guesser must not see it.
  3. Huddle for about 5 seconds. Actors may whisper to assign roles, for example "I'm the pilot, you two are passengers".
  4. Act in silence. The guesser looks up and the timer starts. No talking, humming or sound effects.
  5. Guess. The guesser calls out full guesses. Actors may nod, shake their heads or beckon to show warm or cold.
  6. Score and rotate. One point if the scene is guessed in time. Pick a new guesser and repeat.

Staging: Divide and Conquer

The most common mistake is mirror-miming, where every actor does the same gesture at once. It looks like visual noise. Borrow a few ideas from theater instead:

Set the background

Assign one or two actors to create the setting, such as swaying like trees in a storm or forming the rollercoaster cart.

Create a focal interaction

Have two actors work together or clash, like a chef flipping dough while another rolls pastry.

Use different heights

Let some actors crouch or kneel while others stand, so the guesser can see everyone.

Repeat the key clue

If the guesser is stuck, one actor can make the most telling action bigger and slower instead of everyone changing at once.

Scoring Formats

  • 60-Second Blitz: One guesser stands at the front. The team acts out as many scenes as possible in 60 seconds. Each scene guessed scores 1 point. Rotate guessers for three rounds.
  • Sudden Death Race: Each team gets one scene and a stopwatch counts up. The team whose guesser solves it in the fewest seconds wins the round.

Adapting the Game

With young children

Use the Animals set, give 90 seconds, and let an adult act as one of the team.

In the classroom

Use it as an icebreaker or a review activity. Write your own scenes from the topic being studied.

At work

Mix people from different teams in each group. The shared silly task helps people loosen up.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can actors talk before the round starts?

Yes. Give the team a short huddle of about 5 seconds to assign roles. As soon as the timer starts and the guesser looks up, everyone stays silent.

Can the guesser ask yes-or-no questions?

In the standard rules, no. The guesser calls out guesses and actors may nod, shake their heads or beckon to show how close they are.

How many players do you need?

One guesser and at least three actors. Four to seven actors works best. With eight or more, split into two teams so the space stays clear.

Can we make our own scenes?

Yes. A good scene is a place or situation with at least three roles. Think "airport baggage claim" or "a bakery at 5 a.m.".

Tip for hosts: Reverse Charades works well as the first game of the night. Nobody is singled out, so it relaxes mixed-age groups quickly and gets everyone laughing before the competitive rounds.
